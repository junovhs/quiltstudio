**QuiltStudio — product coherence review and working BrandScript**

November 15, 2026. Revised after repository review, live interface inspection, an experience report from the creator, and his account of his wife's making process. The creator supports his wife, who quilts, and is familiar with quilting concepts through her. Her workflow is reported by him, not directly observed here. Other audience and emotional claims remain hypotheses. The review below records the baseline findings and proposed direction.

**Implementation update:** The integrated implementation now includes a guided idea/design/size/make journey, image-to-editable drafts and tracing, proportional sizing, calibrated camera estimates, protected cut/sewn checkpoints, smaller batches inside panels, inserted joining strips, measured assemblies, remaining-work exports, and the iPhone PWA shell. See [release scope and verification](docs/coherent-making-release.md). The review below records the original evidence and hypotheses. Physical camera accuracy and installed-iPhone behavior still need device validation.

**The product direction I recommend:** help a person bring an imagined quilt into a structured, editable design and keep that design useful as they make and adapt the quilt. Inspiration may be an AI-generated picture, a loose drawing, an existing design, or an idea discovered in the app. Preserve the pleasure of playing with colour and pattern along the way.

Working promise: **“Design a quilt you love, with a clear path to making it.”**

This is a direction for the product to earn. The current app provides design tools, estimates, diagrams, and construction summaries; it has not been established in this review that every supported design produces reliable, complete sewing instructions.

The project has two coherent entry routes into an ongoing process: bring or discover an idea → shape a design → make a batch → observe what exists → adapt the next batch. The typed design connects inspiration and construction throughout that process. Its strongest ingredients are connected: fabric previews, reusable blocks, symmetry, size constraints, calculated pieces, and export. The main weakness is that the interface leaves much of that journey implicit, and the current model does not adequately distinguish a plan from the individual pieces already made.

**The creator's account of the purpose.** He originally built this to help himself and his wife translate AI-generated images or loose drawings into a structured, typed system that can calculate cutting measurements and support refinement. Generating concepts emerged later. His broader ambition is to make quilting accessible, simple, magical, approachable, and largely independent of language. This changes the emphasis of the first review: reference-to-structured-design is a central job, and visual communication is a deliberate product value. Improving clarity must preserve that value.

An important design hypothesis follows: an inspiration image supplies aesthetic intent, but may leave construction ambiguous. The app should help the maker resolve that ambiguity while preserving what they love about the image. A future import flow should distinguish opening an editable .quilt file, displaying a reference image, and interpreting an image into editable pieces. Those are different capabilities. Existing .quilt import does not imply image interpretation.

**First human observation.** The creator tried Random quilt, found a design and colours he liked, and enjoyed seeing it open with selectable elements. His next intention was to set the overall size. He tried the displayed dimensions, discovered that they only toggle a lock, then looked in Settings. The 42-inch fabric width there conflicted with the 58 × 58-inch quilt in his mental model. He reported confusion and frustration at this point, while otherwise describing the experience positively. The device was not reported. This is evidence of a successful inspiration-to-editing handoff followed by a specific resizing failure; it is not evidence that the whole interface needs replacement.

His follow-up clarified the intended behavior: he wanted the quilt four feet wide, expected the design to stay the same, and expected the underlying measurements to update correctly. That establishes proportional whole-quilt resizing as the immediate product requirement for this experience.

**A core workflow: adapt while making.** The creator reports that his wife starts with an approximate overall size and arrangement, such as a blanket roughly three feet wide with three large panels across. She estimates cuts, checks with a ruler when useful, and accepts changes in the resulting size. If a block comes out eight inches rather than the planned ten, she makes its adjoining strip to fit the block when she reaches that step. She cuts batches as needed. Her work remains relative to what already exists.

The product must support this as an ordinary way to quilt. The previous emphasis on resolving the entire plan before beginning was too narrow. Her process demonstrates adaptation and practical judgment; it does not establish anxiety or a desire for stricter instructions. The potential pain to validate is keeping a digital plan and its upcoming cuts useful while the physical quilt evolves.

**Design requirements from this workflow:**

- Treat an approximate overall target, an exact requirement, and a measured piece as different kinds of information. A flexible target may move as the work develops. Preserve an exact requirement when the maker deliberately sets one, and surface conflicts when it cannot coexist with the physical work.
- Express relationships such as “this strip fits this edge” and “these panels form this row.” A change to a measured block should update the appropriate future pieces. It should not automatically shrink every part of the quilt.
- Preserve already-cut and already-sewn material. A proposed adjustment can suggest an explicit physical operation, but must never silently rewrite what the maker actually has. Changing a plan and correcting a recorded measurement are separate actions.
- Distinguish individual physical copies from their shared block design. One eight-inch block does not prove that all copies are eight inches. Allow recording a common measurement for a batch when that is what the maker has.
- Offer a next-batch view tied to a selected block, row, or join. Keep the whole-project cut list available for people who prefer preparing everything in advance. In adaptive work, future dependent cuts remain provisional until the relevant dimensions are known.
- Ask for measurements at useful checkpoints, such as when another piece must fit a completed assembly. Do not require recording every cut or seam. If a calculation depends on an unreported physical change, the app must obtain that measurement or show that it is still using the planned value.
- When a measurement is recorded, make its referent visible: an unsewn cut piece, the current outer edge of a sewn assembly, or the eventual finished dimension. Seam allowances must follow that construction state; do not add them twice.

A small interaction to prototype: select the block that has been made → enter its current measured dimensions on its edges → see the adjoining, uncut strip highlight and its required dimensions update → make the next batch. Existing pieces remain fixed and the overall estimate can change. If a join cannot be resolved by changing unmade pieces, show the conflict and feasible alternatives instead of inventing a fit. This is proposed behavior, not a claim that the current solver supports it.

Visual treatment can distinguish planned pieces from made pieces through outlines, texture, and small pictorial state markers, with optional text and accessible names. The maker should be able to see what exists, what is next, and what will change. Avoid using colour alone or marking an acceptable departure from the original size as an error.

**A concrete resizing interaction to test.** Clicking the displayed quilt dimensions should open sizing; the lock should have its own target. Show the quilt with horizontal and vertical measurement arrows and editable dimensions. Accept familiar expressions such as 48 inches or 4 feet, with explicit units. Keep proportions linked initially: the reported 58 × 58-inch quilt becomes 48 × 48 inches. Preview the same pattern at the new dimensions and allow cancel or undo. This is a proposed interaction, not an implemented feature.

For an entirely unmade design, the behavior underneath must scale finished geometry by 48/58, preserve the selected seam allowance, and recalculate method-specific cuts, counts, and material estimates. Do not multiply existing cut sizes or yardage by the same factor. Exact resizing can produce awkward cut sizes; show those consequences and offer a separate adjustment, rather than silently changing the intended pattern or rounding pieces independently. Let the maker retain the new size as an approximate target or deliberately hold it exact. A specifically locked or physically made block is a separate constraint and needs an explicit resolution if proportional resizing would change it. A prior piece-size anchor must not silently undo the requested quilt resize. Once making has begun, offer adaptations that respect the existing pieces rather than applying unrestricted global scaling.

Fabric width belongs to the source material: depict a fabric roll or strip with a crosswise measurement. Quilt size belongs to the finished object: depict the quilt with edge measurements. Their different physical referents should make the distinction understandable without relying on the English word “width.”

**Camera-assisted measurement is a candidate way to connect the worktable to the design.** The creator proposed live edge detection and measurement. Its strongest purpose here is recording the current dimensions of a selected physical block or assembly, then adapting its unmade neighbours. It can reduce measurement entry while preserving the maker's batch-as-needed workflow. This is a proposed feature; no camera prototype or accuracy validation has been completed.

The camera can supply an image, but an outline in pixels does not by itself supply inches. The proposed method needs visible geometry of known size in the same plane as the flattened fabric: for example, a known cutting-mat grid or a verified square marker. A ruler can work with appropriate capture geometry, but a single known line is not sufficient to resolve arbitrary perspective in two dimensions. Four non-collinear reference points with known planar coordinates allow a perspective mapping; lens distortion, wrinkles, height differences, and uncertain edges still affect accuracy. This design inference follows the planar geometry described in [OpenCV's homography tutorial](https://docs.opencv.org/4.13.0/d9/dab/tutorial_homography.html). Do not infer physical scale from the pattern's intended dimensions, since finding departures from those dimensions is the purpose of the feature.

First experiment: select a block in the design → open the rear camera → place the actual block flat with a known-size reference visible → freeze a frame → inspect or adjust the outline → accept a measurement → preview changes to the adjoining unmade pieces. Prefer the outer edge or a specifically selected joining edge; patterned fabric contains many internal edges that should not be mistaken for the boundary. Support an irregular outline rather than silently forcing every physical block into a perfect rectangle. A scanned outer dimension still needs its construction state identified before calculating seam allowances.

Start with guided still-frame measurement and editable corner/edge handles. A live outline can provide framing feedback, but it should not continually alter the saved quilt while the phone moves. Retain manual measurement entry and a photo-capture/import route when live capture is unavailable. Process frames on the device where practical, load vision code only when needed, and stop camera capture when leaving the tool. Check ruler comparisons across repeated captures, lighting, patterns, camera angles, and the actual iPhones before claiming cutting-level precision. An apparent stable outline or an algorithmic confidence score is not a demonstrated error bound. Never use extra decimal places to imply untested accuracy.

Asked about the existing work surface, the creator suggested instructing users to photograph the piece on a well-lit surface. This is a proposed capture instruction, not confirmation of a gridded mat or ruler in her routine. Lighting helps distinguish the boundary but does not establish real-world scale. A simple capture guide should visually show the flattened piece and a known-size reference together. An existing square template could be a reference if its actual outer dimensions are verified; a tool's nominal finished-size label must not be assumed to equal its physical dimensions. A printed marker also needs a physical size check because printer scaling varies. Evaluate camera setup time and awkwardness as well as numerical error: a feature that takes longer than her ordinary ruler may still help document a complex assembly, but may not improve a simple strip-making step.

**Platform requirement: an installable iPhone PWA, drawing on the sibling app shell.** The creator explicitly identified `/home/juno/dawndishsoap` as the reference. QuiltStudio already has a standalone [manifest](/home/juno/quiltstudio/public/manifest.webmanifest), [service worker](/home/juno/quiltstudio/public/sw.js), and production registration in [main.ts](/home/juno/quiltstudio/src/main.ts). This is a web app launched from the Home Screen; its hardware features must be supported by the browser runtime. Apple describes the standalone Home Screen behavior in [its web-app guidance](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10120/).

Useful sibling foundations inspected: [viewport-layout.ts](/home/juno/dawndishsoap/src/lib/viewport-layout.ts) distinguishes an open keyboard from a stale reduced viewport; [page.tsx](/home/juno/dawndishsoap/src/app/page.tsx:145) resynchronizes layout on orientation, focus, page-show, and visibility events; [globals.css](/home/juno/dawndishsoap/src/app/globals.css:166) keeps the shell and overlays in the usable viewport. Port these patterns into Svelte with the camera and numeric-entry sheets in mind. Test portrait and landscape at the cutting table. The sibling's service worker handles push rather than QuiltStudio-style offline caching, so it is not a replacement to copy wholesale. Its reload-on-new-version behavior also needs adaptation: preserve in-progress design and measurement state before applying an update.

Live video has a standard web implementation path through `getUserMedia`, requiring a secure context and camera permission; a rear-camera preference can be requested. See [the API documentation](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/MediaDevices/getUserMedia). Inline iPhone preview should account for [WebKit's video policies](https://webkit.org/blog/6784/new-video-policies-for-ios/). Validate actual installed Home Screen behavior, permission denial/retry, phone locking, background/foreground transitions, and camera reacquisition. There are [reported Home Screen camera startup issues](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=282327); reports are reasons for device checks, not evidence that every current iPhone fails. Build the measurement experiment around ordinary camera frames and known geometry, without assuming native depth or AR capabilities from the PWA shell.

**Visual communication is a requirement.** My earlier recommendation to add labels was incomplete. Prioritize spatial diagrams, direct manipulation, meaningful previews, highlighted affected copies, and visible before/after states. A lock or scissors icon alone cannot explain every consequence. For construction, show the sequence: starting pieces → sewing line → cut/trim → resulting unit, with dimensions attached to the correct object and stage. Provide concise translatable explanations and accessible names as another way to understand the same interaction. Test comprehension without coaching; do not assume an icon is universal. Language independence also warrants explicit measurement-unit choices.

**Where this project comes from.** [PROJECT.md](/home/juno/quiltstudio/PROJECT.md) records a specific 47-inch floral medallion, purchased checkerboard binding, repeated corner and side blocks, equal-length border strips, and unresolved half-rectangle triangles. Those are excellent examples of real constraints. [The reference inventory](/home/juno/quiltstudio/references/REFERENCE.md) explicitly cautions against deriving exact triangle cutting instructions solely from the schematic. That distinction between a visual design and a construction method should remain visible in the product.

Some personal assumptions have become general defaults: the example quilt, its fabrics, specific cutting guides, and which guides include seam allowance. These make sense for the original maker. A new user's tools and intentions need to be established rather than silently inherited.

**Who to serve first.** The originating use case is a maker and a helper translating an idea into a workable plan. They may contribute different kinds of knowledge: taste, sewing experience, spatial reasoning, or comfort with software. A useful first validation group is people with basic sewing support who want to realize their own designs, including such pairs. Accessibility for absolute beginners remains part of the ambition; it can be developed through verified simple construction paths while retaining depth for experienced makers.

| Situation and desired outcome | Existing support | Missing or uncertain |
|---|---|---|
| “I have fabrics I love. Help me see a quilt in them.” | Fabric colours/photos, palettes, generated layouts, variations | No fabric inventory quantities or directional-print constraints; choosing fabrics does not establish that the user owns enough |
| “I like this idea, but want it to feel like mine.” | Classic blocks, recolouring, repeated blocks, symmetry, rotation | Editing scope is implicit; changing one block definition can affect many visible copies |
| “I know the quilt I want, but need to work out its pieces.” | Nested blocks, measurements, cuts, yardage, diagrams | Manual translation is required; no verified image-to-pattern workflow; some geometries have approximate construction guidance |
| “I need this to work with my dimensions and cutting tools.” | Locks, size adjustment, guide matching, tidy sizes | The effects of adjustments and reasons for refusal need explanation; personal guide defaults may be wrong for someone else |
| “I have started making it. Help me resume accurately.” | Saved projects, cutting/sewing checkmarks, exports | Progress is not tied robustly to changing quantities or design revisions; construction methods and next steps need to be explicit |
| “This block turned out smaller; make the next strip fit it.” | Size adjustment, locks, nested blocks provide some foundations | No complete physical-piece measurement model or next-batch workflow; edits to shared definitions can affect other copies; planned and actual dimensions need separate meaning |
| “I want to enjoy experimenting.” | Immediate visual feedback, undo, generated variations | Saving and comparing favourites could make exploration more satisfying; finishing a physical quilt need not be the goal of every session |

Absolute beginners are a plausible later audience through a small, physically verified set of simple projects. Arbitrary pattern publishing, comprehensive stash management, and advanced construction methods would broaden the promise substantially. They should not become implicit requirements for making the present product useful.

**The likely pain.** The practical difficulty is keeping appearance, dimensions, available fabric, tools, and construction useful as both the design and physical work change. For an adaptive maker, the job may be “help me work out what fits next.” For another maker, it may be “help me follow a precise plan.” Uncertainty about cutting remains a hypothesis for some users, not a description of the creator's wife. Users may arrive through curiosity, pleasure, or a desire to reduce mental bookkeeping. Validate where the app helps without interrupting their flow.

**What to preserve.** The quilt remains the visual focus. People can begin without an account. Repeated blocks let one decision improve a whole design. Fabric photos connect digital choices to tangible material. The generator allows people to express taste by choosing examples. Portable .quilt files include photos, and pictures and printed diagrams support work away from the screen. These features serve autonomy and creative ownership.

**Where coherence breaks, with evidence.**

1. **The first meaningful action is hard to identify.** A first visit opens the medallion in the editor. The narrow live layout is largely icon-based. There is no visible explanation of whose example this is, what to do first, or the route to a personal project. Give newcomers a small choice such as “Explore ideas,” “Adapt a simple quilt,” or “Start from scratch,” with immediate canvas access for returning users. Avoid making orientation a long mandatory tutorial.

2. **The making view hides information needed to act.** [MakeSheet.svelte](/home/juno/quiltstudio/src/components/MakeSheet.svelte:42) puts warnings in a title tooltip, and dimensions are revealed on hover. Fabric names and section meanings are mostly implicit. Construction text already exists as `Cut.how`, but the sheet does not render it. Keep diagrams central: attach quantities and dimensions to the relevant fabric pieces, distinguish cut and finished objects visually, and show the method as a sequence. Provide fabric identifiers and concise localized explanations where useful. Offer explicit, keyboard-accessible completion controls rather than relying on a hidden double-tap gesture.

3. **Construction confidence needs to be specific to the design.** [calc.ts](/home/juno/quiltstudio/src/lib/calc.ts:65) explicitly handles stretched blocks, approximate non-square triangle units, and non-2:1 geese with warnings. Those warnings should appear beside the affected piece, with a route to resolve them. A child-before-parent block order is a useful assembly overview, but does not establish a complete sewing method for arbitrary layouts. Clearly identify which methods have been checked, which need a test block, and which are only estimates. Exports should carry the same assumptions and unresolved issues.

4. **Material quantities need their meaning attached.** The yardage calculation assumes a usable fabric width, selects cutting orientation to reduce length, and adds an allowance. It does not encode print direction or matching requirements. Binding is reported as running length in yards; that should be visibly distinguished from yardage of fabric off a bolt. The making sheet also omits backing information calculated elsewhere. A useful materials list should state what each number means, its assumptions, and what remains for the maker to decide.

5. **Powerful edits need understandable consequences.** Linked block definitions are valuable, but the app should show “This changes all 4 copies” and offer a way to make an independent copy when appropriate. Size adjustments should preview affected pieces and borders. A refused operation currently can communicate only through shaking, as seen in [Canvas.svelte](/home/juno/quiltstudio/src/components/Canvas.svelte:18) and the sizing controls. Explain the constraint and offer the next available action. Distinguish guide mismatch, awkward measurement, and unsupported construction; they do not all mean the design is wrong.

6. **A finished checklist item can become misleading after editing.** Source inspection shows that cut/unit progress keys include identity and size but not quantity, while block progress uses only block ID. If a requirement changes from four identical pieces to eight, the prior completion flag can remain. A block can also change while retaining its checked state. See [calc.ts](/home/juno/quiltstudio/src/lib/calc.ts:30) and [MakeSheet.svelte](/home/juno/quiltstudio/src/components/MakeSheet.svelte:17). Treat this as a source-derived finding, not a reproduced sewing session. Record completed counts and the plan revision, and explain additional work after a design change. Physical work cannot be undone like a canvas edit.

7. **The saving promise exceeds what is preserved across devices.** [Account.svelte](/home/juno/quiltstudio/src/components/Account.svelte:98) says an account keeps quilts safe and on all devices. [cloud.svelte.ts](/home/juno/quiltstudio/src/lib/cloud.svelte.ts:6) documents that photos remain on the device and newest-copy conflict resolution is used. Local storage writes also suppress failures. Show whether the design and photos are saved, pending, or device-only; explain how to export a complete copy. Consider recoverable versions for conflicting edits. Do not imply a tested backup guarantee from the existence of sync.

**The generator's role.** It is well placed to help people recognize what they like. In the live interface, selecting a picture produces related options, while a separate “Use” action opens a project. Make those two actions explicit, especially on narrow screens where the explanatory tip is hidden. “Explore quilt ideas” better expresses the benefit than “Random quilt.” Retain playful surprise as an option. In a later iteration, let practical constraints influence suggestions: techniques the maker can use, minimum piece size, and material availability. Current scoring measures visual contrast and busyness; it should not be presented as a judgment of sewability or personal taste.

**StoryBrand worksheet.** StoryBrand's BrandScript has seven narrative elements, with an additional character-transformation exercise. The customer is the central character and the product serves as guide. This worksheet follows the categories in the [official StoryBrand materials](https://storybrand.com/downloads/basb/Building-a-StoryBrand-Audio-Book-Images.pdf). Its QuiltStudio answers are proposed positioning, not validated user research.

| Element | Working answer for QuiltStudio |
|---|---|
| 1. Character and desire | A person, sometimes working with a more experienced maker or a helper, wants to turn a quilt idea into something they can make and feel proud of. The idea may start as a picture, sketch, or discovery in the app. |
| 2. Problem | External: the picture, the measurements, and the pieces on the table can diverge; each change affects what fits next. Internal hypothesis: “I want to keep creating without working everything out again.” Philosophical: a quilting tool should respect the maker's language, judgment, and way of working. Antagonist: the work of keeping an evolving idea and its construction connected. |
| 3. Guide | Empathy: “A quilt can change as you make it.” Authority to demonstrate: calculations that respect actual pieces, clear visual relationships, and construction paths checked in fabric. Preserve the maker's judgment and make tradeoffs visible. The original quilt provides context, but is not proof that all generated designs are reliable. |
| 4. Plan | Bring or discover an idea → shape the quilt → make a batch and adapt what comes next. Agreement to earn: preserve intent and existing work, update dependent measurements, keep digital edits reversible, and show unresolved construction issues. The bring-an-image and adaptive-making routes need development; editable file import already exists. |
| 5. Action | Direct: “Start my quilt.” Low-commitment: “Try a sample” or “Explore ideas.” Within a project: “Review my cutting plan.” Signup should follow a demonstrated need to save or move devices. |
| 6. Avoidable failure | Cutting future batches to an obsolete plan, losing track of what fits, unnecessary rework, or losing a design. A quilt that ends up larger or smaller than an approximate target is not inherently a failure. |
| 7. Success | A quilt the maker loves, with a design that remains useful as they work and clear choices for what to make next. Success can include adapting the original dimensions. A satisfying exploratory session is also valid. |

Transformation: **“I have to keep the picture, the maths, and the changing pieces in my head” → “I can see my quilt take shape and work out what fits next.”** This is proposed positioning to validate, not an attributed statement from the maker.

Proposed long-term one-liner: “QuiltStudio helps you shape a quilt idea and work out what to make next, as the quilt grows and changes.” Adaptive behavior must be implemented and checked before this becomes a claim about the shipped product.

Proposed opening copy: “Design a quilt you love.” Supporting sentence: “Explore patterns with your fabrics, adjust the design, and review what you'll need to make it.” Primary action: “Start my quilt.” Secondary action: “Try a sample.” Use stronger promises about reliable cutting instructions only where the construction workflow has earned them.

The product already expresses creative agency well through direct manipulation and variations. Its weakest StoryBrand elements are the guide and plan: empathy, explanations, and a clear next step are largely absent from the editor. Improving those means improving the experience itself, beyond changing homepage language.

**What I would prioritize.** First address the observed break: a person likes a generated design and wants to make it a specific size. Implement and test the sizing interaction described above, including correct recalculation and explicit handling of constraints. Repeat the creator's task without coaching. Success means he can make the quilt four feet wide, sees the design preserved, and understands the resulting dimensions.

Pair this with an adaptive-making prototype: record a made block's current dimensions and update an adjoining uncut strip, without changing other made pieces. Validate this small interaction against the wife's batch-as-needed workflow. Then establish one small end-to-end project that a person can personalize, make in batches, and adapt successfully. Check construction and the progress-after-editing problem against fabric. Next validate translating a real sketch or AI-generated reference alongside the maker. Use those observations to choose useful reference-import aids. Make returning trustworthy through saving status, photo portability, plan versions, and progress that corresponds to physical work.

**How to judge whether it serves people.** Observe whether users can make an intended change without coaching, explain what changed, distinguish cut and finished dimensions, identify unresolved construction questions, and resume without losing their place. Ask whether they feel ownership, enjoyment, and appropriate confidence. For making sessions, check actual test-block dimensions and whether instructions caused rework. For exploration, ask whether the user found a direction they like. More time in the app or more generated quilts is not inherently better.

**Using human experience to revise this review.** The creator has supplied the origin story and completed the first task. He is a knowledgeable helper and the app's creator, not the primary quilter. His wife has not yet been interviewed in this review. Continue with one task at a time, without explaining the controls first:

- Completed first task: finding a pleasing generated design succeeded; overall sizing blocked further progress. Completed clarification: four feet wide, same design, recalculated measurements. The cutting-plan experience remains untested by this participant.
- Additional report from the creator: his wife works from approximate overall dimensions, adapts to actual results, and cuts batches as needed; an adjoining strip is made to fit the block that exists at that stage. This establishes adaptive making as a required workflow. The exact seam/measurement conventions and physical fit have not been directly observed.
- Next useful observation, when both participants are available: watch one transition from an existing block to its adjoining strip. Record what she looks at, what she measures, and what decisions she makes. Test whether recording that one meaningful measurement and showing the next batch helps or interrupts her.
- After a resize prototype exists: repeat the four-foot task without explaining how to use it. Observe whether the result matches the participant's expectation and whether source-fabric width is still confused with quilt dimensions.
- Later construction check: have a quilter explain the printed plan for one simple block in their own words. Only after the plan is checked and the person agrees, make a sample with expendable fabric and compare the result with the intended dimensions.

Keep observations separate from interpretations and requests. “I tapped the swatch and the colour didn't change” is an observation; “the model of selection is unclear” is an interpretation; “add a tutorial” is one possible intervention. Choose the smallest intervention that addresses the observed problem, and repeat the same task afterward.

An owner's experience is valuable evidence, including about goals and taste, but familiarity can conceal first-use friction. Eventually repeat the core task with a few people at different quilting skill levels. Do not treat this first review or one participant's reaction as a verdict for everyone.
